# Wildfire Prevention Literature Review

A method-focused review supporting the MSDS696 practicum project. Its purpose is to **justify and refine the analytic choices** in this project by grounding them in precedent — not to establish topical novelty. For each major methodological decision, the review asks: *has this been done before, how, and what does that precedent tell me about doing it here?*

**Project research questions:**
1. **(Descriptive)** Across a set of contrasting U.S. region-seasons, which wildfire causes (natural and human) drive the most burned area, and do those patterns differ enough to demand different prevention and mitigation strategies?
2. **(Predictive)** Can a next-season cause-risk profile — the expected composition of causes for a region and upcoming season, ranked by the burned area each is expected to drive — be predicted well enough to pre-target that prevention effort?

## Data sources

Short, K. C. (2022). *Spatial wildfire occurrence data for the United States, 1992–2020 [FPA_FOD_20221014]* (6th ed.) [Data set]. Forest Service Research Data Archive. https://doi.org/10.2737/RDS-2013-0009.6

> The Fire Program Analysis fire-occurrence database (FPA-FOD) compiles ~2.3 million U.S. wildfire records from 1992–2020, drawn from federal, state, and local reporting systems. Each record includes location, discovery date, final fire size, and a statistical cause classification.

U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. (2025). *Level III and IV ecoregions of the continental United States* [Data set]. https://www.epa.gov/eco-research/level-iii-and-iv-ecoregions-continental-united-states

> A GIS dataset delineating the Level III and IV ecoregions of the continental United States, based on the Omernik ecoregion framework. Ecoregions denote areas of general similarity in ecosystems and environmental resources.

## Literature

Edgeley, C. M., Evans, A. M., Devenport, S. E., Kohler, G., Zamudio, Z. M., & DeGrandpre, W. D. (2025). Preventing human-caused wildfire ignitions on public lands: A review of best practices. *Forest Science, 71*(6), 493–521. https://doi.org/10.1007/s44391-025-00025-9

> A comprehensive (not systematic) review of 90 publications, 1937–2023, on interventions to reduce accidental or negligent human-caused wildfire ignitions on U.S. public lands, organized around the NWCG "fire prevention triangle" of education, enforcement, engineering, and administration. Findings are grouped by these four categories, each closing with a table of reported best practices, and the review concludes with a set of research gaps. The authors report that no single prevention approach eradicates human-caused ignitions, that effectiveness depends on tailoring interventions to local context and specific user groups, and that most of the underlying social science predates 1990 and is concentrated in California national forests.

Prestemon, J. P., Hawbaker, T. J., Bowden, M., Carpenter, J., Brooks, M. T., Abt, K. L., Sutphen, R., & Scranton, S. (2013). *Wildfire ignitions: A review of the science and recommendations for empirical modeling* (General Technical Report SRS-171). U.S. Department of Agriculture, Forest Service, Southern Research Station. https://www.srs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/gtr/gtr_srs171.pdf

> A USDA Forest Service technical report, developed under the National Cohesive Wildland Fire Management Strategy, that reviews the science on the underlying causes of wildfire ignitions and the role of prevention across natural, accidental, and incendiary (arson) fire categories. It presents a conceptual model linking ignitions to biophysical drivers, societal drivers, wildfire prevention, and fire management, then surveys the biophysical, societal, and management variables used to predict ignitions and the reported spatial and temporal patterns and trends in ignition frequency and cause. It compiles federal wildfire-cause categories and their shares of ignitions and area burned, and closes with recommendations for building empirically based ignition models across spatial and temporal scales.

Davis, K. T., Peeler, J., Fargione, J., Haugo, R. D., Metlen, K. L., Robles, M. D., & Woolley, T. (2024). Tamm review: A meta-analysis of thinning, prescribed fire, and wildfire effects on subsequent wildfire severity in conifer dominated forests of the Western US. *Forest Ecology and Management, 561*, 121885. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foreco.2024.121885

> A meta-analysis of peer-reviewed studies and technical reports on how mechanical thinning, prescribed burning, and prior wildfire affect the severity of subsequent wildfires in conifer-dominated forests of the western United States. From 220 publications the authors screened 40 meeting four inclusion criteria (empirical severity data with contained controls, post-treatment wildfire, defined severity measures, and specified treatments), then used a log response ratio effect size to answer three questions: how much treatments reduce severity within treated areas, how effects vary by treatment type/age and forest type, and how fire weather moderates them. It reports that mechanical thinning with prescribed or pile burning and prescribed burning alone reduced subsequent severity by roughly 62–72% relative to untreated areas, that thinning alone was less effective, that effects did not vary by forest type but declined over time, and that prior wildfire had more variable effects.

Stephens, J., Joseph, M., Bitters, M. E., Iglesias, V., Tuff, T., Mahood, A., Rangwala, I., Wolken, J., O'Connor, C. D., & Balch, J. K. (2026). Fires of unusual size: Future of extreme and emerging wildfire in a warming United States (2020–2060). *Fire, 9*(5), 208. https://doi.org/10.3390/fire9050208

> A study projecting future wildfire occurrence, maximum fire size, and total area burned for large fires (>1000 acres) across all EPA Level III ecoregions of the contiguous United States for 2020–2060. The authors apply Bayesian statistical models (developed by Joseph et al., 2019) trained on historical fire records from the Monitoring Trends and Burn Severity (MTBS) program for 1984–2019, integrating projected population density under the SSP2 scenario and downscaled climate simulations from eight global climate models under the RCP 4.5 emissions scenario. Predictors include air temperature, housing density, precipitation, relative humidity, and wind speed, with spatially varying non-linear effects and spatiotemporal random effects nested across EPA Level I, II, and III ecoregions; maximum fire size is identified as the 90th percentile of fires within each Level III ecoregion. Relative to 1984–2019, the authors project nationwide increases of 56% in fire occurrence, 59% in area burned, and 63% in maximum fire size, with fire occurrence increasing most strongly in the eastern U.S. (including regions where large fires have historically been rare) and western ecoregions experiencing the largest absolute increases in burned area and extreme fire size.

Omernik, J. M., & Griffith, G. E. (2014). Ecoregions of the conterminous United States: Evolution of a hierarchical spatial framework. *Environmental Management, 54*(6), 1249–1266. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00267-014-0364-1

> A profile article describing the concept, mapping approach, and evolution of the EPA/USGS ecoregion framework for the conterminous United States. It traces the framework from its first 1987 map into a hierarchical structure of four nested levels — Level I (10 ecoregions), Level II, Level III, and Level IV (967 ecoregions) — developed in collaboration with agencies and institutions in the United States, Canada, and Mexico. The authors explain the underlying "weight-of-evidence" pattern-analysis method, in which ecoregions are delineated from the spatial correlation of many biotic and abiotic factors (soils, vegetation, geology, physiography, climate, hydrology, land use, and human activity) rather than a single characteristic, and discuss the ongoing debate over how ecosystems and ecoregions should be defined and whether qualitative or quantitative delineation approaches are more defensible. It presents worked examples of how Level III and IV regions were distinguished in the Great Plains and the North American Deserts, and includes a table of selected state, federal, and other applications of the framework spanning water-quality standards, wildlife and conservation strategies, land-cover change analysis, and nitrogen-deposition assessment. The article reports that the framework's defensibility rests on its usefulness across many applications and notes that the capacities and tolerances of ecoregions change differently from region to region, with implications for environmental management under land-use and climate change.
